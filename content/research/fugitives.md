## What you see

1. **A row of 13 plaster body casts** displayed in a single line inside a glass-fronted case/shelter at the rear (southeast corner) of the garden — figures frozen in poses of collapse, curling, and reaching, showing the final moments of people trying to flee. Originally found in three smaller clusters, they are now shown together in one row. [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives); [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/garden-of-the-fugitives/)
2. **An open garden/vineyard plot**, not a house interior — confirmed: this area was a working vineyard and vegetable garden before AD 79, with a pergola-covered triclinium for outdoor dining at its edge, not a residential space. It has been carefully replanted to reflect its ancient planting scheme. [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives)
3. **Visible ash/pumice stratigraphy** in the excavation faces around the garden — the casts were recovered "near the top of the volcanic fill," meaning the victims were caught after a significant depth of pumice had already accumulated. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R1/1%2021%2006.htm); [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives)
4. **The boundary/perimeter wall** of the vineyard enclosure separating it from the adjoining house and the Vicolo della Nave Europa (the lane on the western side). [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R1/1%2021%2006.htm)
5. **Two small, barely-preserved "servant rooms"** (numbered 13 and 14) in the southeast corner of the garden — "hardly anything remains" of them structurally, but their existence is documented. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R1/1%2021%2006.htm)
6. **The Fiorelli plaster-cast technique itself is on display**, not just its results: these casts were made by pouring liquid plaster into the human-shaped voids left in the hardened ash after the bodies decomposed — a technique invented by archaeologist Giuseppe Fiorelli in 1863. **These are original human remains** (bones and decayed-flesh residue are embedded inside the plaster) cast in their original findspot — not replicas. [pompeiisites.org press release, "The Plaster Casts of Pompeii"](https://pompeiisites.org/en/comunicati/the-plaster-casts-of-pompeii-an-itinerary-through-the-eruption-of-ad-79/)
7. **Note on relocation/conservation:** while the casts remain displayed at the Garden itself, Pompeii Archaeological Park has, since March 2026, opened a dedicated exhibition of 22 selected plaster casts (drawn from the roughly 100 that exist across the site) at the Palestra Grande — visitors should be aware some Pompeii casts elsewhere have been moved for conservation/display, though the Garden of the Fugitives' own casts are presented in situ at the garden. [pompeiisites.org press release](https://pompeiisites.org/en/comunicati/the-plaster-casts-of-pompeii-an-itinerary-through-the-eruption-of-ad-79/)
8. Root cavities of ancient vines are part of the broader garden-archaeology method pioneered at Pompeii (Wilhelmina Jashemski's "root casting" technique), used across Pompeii's gardens/vineyards generally; not independently confirmed as currently visible/marked specifically inside this garden plot in the sources checked — flagged as **unconfirmed for this specific site**. [Wikipedia EN, Wilhelmina Jashemski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelmina_Jashemski)

## History in layers

### (a) 30-second intro
Before Vesuvius erupted, this quiet plot just inside the Nocera Gate was a working vineyard, not a house. On the morning of the eruption's second day, a deadly volcanic surge caught a group of people here as they tried to run for safety. Thirteen of them never made it — and their final moments are still visible today, cast in plaster exactly where they fell.

### (b) ~150 words — before and during
Before AD 79, this area at the southeastern edge of the city, just inside the Porta di Nocera, was not residential — the northwestern end of the insula held small houses, but most of the plot had been converted into a commercial vineyard and vegetable garden, complete with a pergola-shaded triclinium for outdoor dining. [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives) By the time of the eruption's second, more violent phase, a preceding ~18-20 hour Plinian pumice fall had already blanketed the area roughly 2.8–3.5 m deep. The lethal pyroclastic surges that followed — surges 4 and 5 in the stratigraphic sequence, with emplacement temperatures of roughly 180–260°C — arrived fast and hot enough to kill "in a fraction of a second." [Wikipedia EN, Eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD) The group here was caught "above the layer of pumice" while trying to reach the Nocera Gate. Excavation of the garden and its casts took place in **1961–1962**, with further work in **1973–1974**, under Pompeii superintendent Amedeo Maiuri; the casts were made using the plaster-infill method invented by Giuseppe Fiorelli in 1863. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/garden-of-the-fugitives/); [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives); [pompeiisites.org press release](https://pompeiisites.org/en/comunicati/the-plaster-casts-of-pompeii-an-itinerary-through-the-eruption-of-ad-79/)

### (c) ~250-word deep-dive — study, debate, significance
Maiuri's original 1961 account (published in *National Geographic*, November 1961, as "Last Moments of the Pompeians") described the 13 victims as forming identifiable family/social groups: a merchant showing signs of severe osteoarthritis and old arm fractures; a mother found lying beside her youngest child, near two boys (roughly age two and five by dental assessment) said to have died holding hands; a very young child — about 12–14 months old by dental development, called by some accounts the youngest victim recovered anywhere at Pompeii; and a mid-teenage servant. [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives) Modern scholarship treats parts of Maiuri's narrative with caution: later X-ray analysis showed that what Maiuri had described as a bag carried by the "servant" was in fact a plaster mis-formation, not an object — a reminder that early interpretive storytelling about these casts sometimes outran the physical evidence. [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives) Broader Pompeii-wide research has increasingly used CT scanning on plaster casts to look inside them without breaking them open, and pompeiisites.org's own March 2026 exhibition on the casts explicitly incorporates CAT-scan imaging into its interpretation. Separately, a 2024 ancient-DNA study on other Pompeii cast groups (notably the "House of the Golden Bracelet") overturned assumptions about a presumed mother-and-children "family," showing at least one individual was male and biologically unrelated to the others — a caution that traditional family narratives assigned to Pompeii cast groups, potentially including here, may not hold up to genetic testing. No DNA study specific to the Garden of the Fugitives casts was found in the sources checked for this brief — **flagged as unconfirmed/absence of evidence, not evidence of absence**. In May 2026, pompeiisites.org director Gabriel Zuchtriegel published new findings (originally objects recovered in 2016 restoration work) identifying one Garden of the Fugitives victim as probably a doctor, based on an associated bronze-rimmed medical case with a grinding slab and six surgical instruments (see Anecdotes). [artnet, 19 May 2026, via cached text](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/pompeii-doctor-garden-of-fugitives-2774645) The site's vineyard context has also fed into Wilhelmina Jashemski's pioneering garden-archaeology method (root-cavity casting, pollen/seed analysis) used across Pompeii to reconstruct ancient viticulture, though no source found confirms root-cavity casts are specifically preserved/displayed in this exact plot. [Wikipedia EN, Wilhelmina Jashemski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelmina_Jashemski)

## The detail most visitors miss

Look closely at the case display: what Amedeo Maiuri's 1961 excavation report dramatically described as a bag slung over the shoulder of the "servant" figure (part of the story that this individual was leading the family to safety) was later shown by X-ray analysis to be nothing but an air pocket / plaster mis-formation — not a real object at all. It's a small, concrete example of how the earliest, most vivid storytelling about these casts has since been corrected by later forensic reexamination. [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives)

## Anecdotes

1. **The "doctor's kit" (2026 announcement, sourced, not legend):** Among the Garden of the Fugitives victims, restoration work (objects recovered in 2016, findings published May 2026) uncovered a bronze-rimmed medical case containing a basalt grinding slab and six metal surgical instruments, along with a cloth bag holding seven coins (four silver, three bronze) dated to the Flavian era (AD 69–96) — evidence that one of the dead here may have been a doctor. Park director Gabriel Zuchtriegel: "The site is a powerful testimony to daily life in Pompeii and how its humanity was suddenly broken." Researchers plan to use CT scan data to virtually reconstruct the full medical kit. **Note:** this specific news item could not be independently cross-verified beyond the cited article and its citation on Wikipedia's reference list, due to source access limits during research — treat as a well-sourced but singly-sourced recent claim. [artnet news, 19 May 2026](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/pompeii-doctor-garden-of-fugitives-2774645); cited at [Wikipedia EN, References](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives)
2. **The mother and two boys "holding hands":** Maiuri's original account described a mother found near her youngest child, with two other boys (about ages two and five) that he said had been found holding hands as they fell together. This is Maiuri's own interpretive account from the 1961 excavation/publication — presented by the source as the excavator's narrative framing rather than as independently confirmed forensic fact, so guides should flag the "holding hands" detail as **excavator's account**, not certain. [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives)
3. **Youngest victim at Pompeii (claim, sourced but flagged):** One of the casts here, a child of about 12–14 months old by dental assessment, is described in sourced material as possibly "the youngest victim recovered in Pompeii" — a striking claim worth flagging to visitors as reported rather than definitively established site-wide fact. [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives)

## FAQ

**Q1: Are these real human bodies?**
Yes. The plaster fills a void left in the hardened volcanic ash after the victim's body decomposed, so each cast contains the actual skeleton and any surviving organic residue of the real person, encased in plaster — these are not replicas or sculptures. [pompeiisites.org press release, "The Plaster Casts of Pompeii"](https://pompeiisites.org/en/comunicati/the-plaster-casts-of-pompeii-an-itinerary-through-the-eruption-of-ad-79/)

**Q2: How were the casts made?**
Archaeologist Giuseppe Fiorelli developed the technique in 1863: when excavators found a hollow cavity in the compacted ash, they drilled in and poured liquid plaster of Paris, which filled and preserved the exact shape of the body (including clothing folds and facial expression) before the surrounding ash was carefully removed. [pompeiisites.org press release](https://pompeiisites.org/en/comunicati/the-plaster-casts-of-pompeii-an-itinerary-through-the-eruption-of-ad-79/); [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives)

**Q3: How many people died here, and are there really 13?**
Both pompeiisites.org (official) and Wikipedia state **13 victim casts** are displayed here, discovered mainly in the 1961–1962 excavation (with further excavation in 1973–1974). Note: one 2026 secondary source (artnet, reporting on a May 2026 announcement) refers to "14 people discovered" / "14 hollow cavities" in the same garden — this may reflect a newly attributed 14th cavity/object-find not resulting in a full body cast, or a discrepancy in reporting. **This is flagged as a disputed/uncertain point** — the guide should state 13 as the well-established, officially confirmed figure, while noting some 2026 press coverage cites 14. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/garden-of-the-fugitives/); [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives); [artnet, 19 May 2026, via cached text](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/pompeii-doctor-garden-of-fugitives-2774645)

**Q4: Why is this called a "garden," not a house?**
Because before the eruption this plot really was a working vineyard and vegetable garden (with an outdoor dining pergola), not a residential building — one of several commercial garden/vineyard plots archaeologists have identified within Pompeii's walls. [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_the_Fugitives)

## Hebrew naming

- **Orto dei Fuggiaschi** → אורטו דיי פוג'אסקי (transliteration of the Italian; literal meaning: "גן הנמלטים" / "גן הבורחים" — "Garden of the Fugitives/Escapees")
- **Garden of the Fugitives** (EN, commonly used by English-language guides) → "גן הנמלטים" (Gan HaNimlatim) — this Hebrew calque is the natural rendering and likely the form used by Israeli guides, since it translates the commonly-used English name rather than the Italian.
- **Plaster casts (the Fiorelli method)** → תבניות גבס / יציקות גבס (Tavniyot Gevess / Yetzikot Gevess) — "plaster casts/molds." The **method** itself: "שיטת פיורלי" (Shitat Fiorelli) — "the Fiorelli method," named for Giuseppe Fiorelli (ג'וזפה פיורלי).
- **Pyroclastic surge/flow** → זרם פירוקלסטי (Zerem Pyroklasti) for "pyroclastic flow/surge" generally; more precisely "גל פירוקלסטי" (Gal Pyroklasti) is sometimes used for "surge" specifically (a fast-moving turbulent ash-gas cloud) as distinct from a slower flow. Both terms transliterate the Greek-derived scientific term rather than translating it, which matches common usage among Israeli geology/archaeology guides.
- **Vesuvius eruption AD 79** → התפרצות הר וזוב בשנת 79 לספירה (Hitpartzut Har Vezuv beshnat 79 lasfira)
- **Vineyard** → כרם (Kerem); **Nocera Gate** (Porta di Nocera) → שער נוצֵ'רה (Sha'ar Nochera)
