## What you see

1. **A vast oval bowl set into an earthen embankment**, not freestanding — the seating rings (cavea) are banked directly against mounded excavated soil rather than resting on a full ring of masonry vaulting. Overall footprint ~135 m long × 104 m wide. Source: [Wikipedia EN — Amphitheatre of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheatre_of_Pompeii)
2. **Twin monumental exterior staircases** on the west side (including a central double staircase) climbing the outside of the earthen bank to reach the upper seating tiers directly — a structural shortcut only possible because there is no internal ring-corridor system like later amphitheatres. Source: [pompeiiinpictures.com — Pompeii II.6](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R2/2%2006%2000%20p1.htm)
3. **Three seating tiers (cavea) reflecting Pompeian social hierarchy**: the *ima cavea* (lowest, closest to the arena) for prominent citizens and magistrates — who had special double-width honorific seats — the *media cavea* for the general free citizenry, and the *summa cavea* at the top, where sources note women were likely confined to boxed sections holding up to 14 people each. Source: [pompeiiinpictures.com — Pompeii II.6](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R2/2%2006%2000%20p1.htm)
4. **The sunken arena floor**, roughly 66.7 m × 35.1 m, set about 6 m below the surrounding ground level — dug out and the spoil piled up to form the surrounding seating bank. Source: [Wikipedia EN — Amphitheatre of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheatre_of_Pompeii)
5. **Two axial arena gates**: one at each end of the arena's long axis — one for the ceremonial entry procession of fighters, the other nicknamed (in secondary literature) the "death gate," used to remove the dead and wounded. Source: [pompeiiinpictures.com — Pompeii II.6](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R2/2%2006%2000%20p1.htm)
6. **A decorated parapet wall around the arena edge**, separating spectators from the fighting floor, which the official park site describes as carrying painted decoration of gladiators plus the builders' dedicatory inscriptions. Source: [pompeiisites.org — Amphitheater](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/amphitheater/)
7. **Builder inscriptions naming the two duoviri (magistrates) who funded construction with private money: Caius (Gaius) Quinctius Valgus and Marcus Porcius** — the latter reportedly a relative of Cato the Younger. Consistently confirmed across sources: EN Wikipedia, IT Wikipedia ("Gaio Quinzio Valgo e Marco Porcio"), and the official park page ("Magistrates Caius Quinctius Valgus and Marcus Porcius"). Sources: [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheatre_of_Pompeii), [Wikipedia IT](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anfiteatro_di_Pompei), [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/amphitheater/)
8. **What's original vs. restored / damaged:** the earthwork core, arena floor, and most of the standing seating structure are ancient (built ~70 BC). The structure suffered earthquake damage in AD 62, was fully buried by the AD 79 eruption (excavated 1748, then fully 1813–1816), and was **damaged by Allied bombing in 1943** during WWII; subsequent conservation work has repaired war damage over the decades. Source: [pompeiiinpictures.com — Pompeii II.6](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R2/2%2006%2000%20p1.htm) (bombing date). I found no dedicated official page detailing exactly which sections were rebuilt — flagged as **thin sourcing**; the 1943 bombing fact itself is corroborated only by pompeiiinpictures.com, not by the official park site or Wikipedia in the pages I checked.
9. **The riot fresco is NOT displayed at the amphitheatre itself.** English Wikipedia states the fresco depicting the AD 59 riot is "now in National Archaeological Museum, Naples" (MANN). I could not independently confirm a specific room number or inventory number for it in the sources I was able to reach (MANN's own site returned a 404 for the pages I tried). **Flagged as moderately thin sourcing on the exact current display location/room within MANN** — the museum attribution itself (MANN, Naples) is corroborated only by the English Wikipedia article; recommend a caveat in the script ("held at the Naples Archaeological Museum") rather than naming a specific gallery room.

## History in layers

### (a) 30-second intro
This oval bowl dug into the earth is one of the oldest stone amphitheatres to survive from the Roman world, built around 70 BC — over a century before Rome's Colosseum. In AD 59 it was the scene of a deadly riot between Pompeians and visiting fans from nearby Nuceria during a gladiator show, so violent that the Roman Senate banned games here for ten years. It's a reminder that Pompeii's entertainment culture could turn as lethal as anything in the arena itself.

### (b) ~150 words
Built around 70 BC by two local magistrates, Caius Quinctius Valgus and Marcus Porcius, using their own money, the amphitheatre is counted among the oldest surviving Roman amphitheatres and among the earliest built in stone. It held roughly 20,000 spectators — sources range from "around 20,000" (Wikipedia) to "up to 20,000" (official park site) to "20,000–24,000" (pompeiiinpictures) — more than Pompeii's entire estimated resident population, drawing crowds from the wider region. In AD 59, a gladiatorial show sponsored by Livineius Regulus sparked a brawl between local Pompeians and rival spectators from Nuceria; per Tacitus, the fighting caused deaths and many wounded, and the Roman Senate punished Pompeii with a ten-year ban on gladiatorial games there, later lifted early after the amphitheatre was damaged in the AD 62 earthquake and rebuilt. The structure was then buried whole by the AD 79 eruption of Vesuvius and first partly excavated in 1748, fully cleared in 1813–1816.

### (c) ~250-word deep-dive
Architecturally, the amphitheatre's most-cited innovation is what it lacks: unlike Rome's later Colosseum, it has **no hypogeum** — no underground network of chambers and lift shafts beneath the arena for staging animals and fighters. Instead, support structures (a quadriporticus) sat above ground near the arena, a simpler, earlier solution reflecting the building's early date in the development of the amphitheatre as a building type. (Source: Wikipedia EN, cross-checked against pompeiiinpictures — both agree on the absence of substructures; treat "earliest known stone amphitheatre in the Roman world" as a commonly repeated but not universally uncontested superlative, since dating priority among early Campanian amphitheatres — e.g., at Capua — is debated among specialists, and neither the sources I reached explicitly resolved this rivalry claim.)

The AD 59 riot is documented by the historian Tacitus in his *Annals*, Book 14, chapter 17 — both the English and Italian Wikipedia articles cite this reference independently. Tacitus records that the brawl began with mutual jeering between Pompeian and Nucerian spectators, escalated into stone-throwing and then weapons, leaving many Nucerians wounded or killed; Italian Wikipedia adds that the games' sponsor, Livineius Regulus, and other instigators were exiled by senatorial decree. A wall painting recovered from a Pompeian house depicts the melee spilling out from the arena into the surrounding porticoes and awning structure — a rare ancient visual record of a real, dated riot — and is now held at Naples' National Archaeological Museum (MANN), per English Wikipedia (exact gallery/inventory number not independently verified in this research pass).

I did not find independently sourced information in the pages fetched about gladiator graffiti physically at the amphitheatre itself (well-known Pompeian gladiator graffiti, such as fan messages about star fighters, are generally associated with the gladiator barracks near the Large Theatre, not this building — this distinction should be kept clear in the script to avoid misattributing barracks graffiti to the amphitheatre). I found no dedicated official material on 2020–2026 restoration or research campaigns specific to this structure within the sources I could reach — **flagged as thin sourcing**; worth a targeted follow-up check of pompeiisites.org's press/news archive closer to the visit date.

## The detail most visitors miss

At the west-side entrance area, look for the **"death gate"** (as labeled in archaeological photo-documentation) — one of the two axial arena gates, on the opposite end from the ceremonial parade entrance, through which the bodies of the dead and wounded gladiators were removed during the games. It's a small, unglamorous doorway easy to walk past, but it is the physical trace of the arena's actual function, not just its architecture. Source: [pompeiiinpictures.com — Pompeii II.6](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R2/2%2006%2000%20p1.htm)

## Anecdotes

1. **The AD 59 Pompeii–Nuceria riot (fact, not legend).** During a gladiatorial show put on by Livineius Regulus, trash-talk between Pompeian home fans and visiting Nucerian spectators turned into stone-throwing, then into an armed brawl inside the amphitheatre, leaving Nucerians dead and wounded on both sides. The Roman Senate responded by banning gladiatorial games at Pompeii for ten years and exiling those responsible for organizing the violence, including Livineius Regulus. Source: Tacitus, *Annals* 14.17, as reported in [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheatre_of_Pompeii) and [Wikipedia IT](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anfiteatro_di_Pompei).
2. **A political motive behind the riot (scholarly interpretation, not a "fact" carved in stone).** Italian Wikipedia notes a likely underlying cause: Nuceria Alfaterna had become a Roman colony in AD 57, which allowed Nucerians to acquire land previously controlled by Pompeians — a real-estate and status grievance that scholars suggest fed the arena confrontation two years later. Label this explicitly as historical interpretation/context rather than a fact stated outright by Tacitus. Source: [Wikipedia IT — Anfiteatro di Pompei](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anfiteatro_di_Pompei)
3. **The ban was lifted early by disaster, not diplomacy (fact).** The ten-year ban imposed after the riot was still in effect when the AD 62 earthquake badly damaged the amphitheatre; the magistrates Cuspius Pansa and his son led the rebuilding, and the ban was revoked once repairs were made — meaning it was a natural disaster, not the passage of time, that effectively ended Pompeii's punishment. Source: [Wikipedia EN — Amphitheatre of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheatre_of_Pompeii), [Wikipedia IT — Anfiteatro di Pompei](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anfiteatro_di_Pompei)

## FAQ

1. **Is this really the oldest amphitheatre in the Roman world?** It is commonly described as one of the oldest surviving Roman amphitheatres and among the earliest built in stone (~70 BC), but flag this as a widely repeated claim rather than an uncontested scholarly consensus — the sources checked did not resolve debates over similarly early amphitheatres elsewhere in Campania. Source: [Wikipedia EN — Amphitheatre of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheatre_of_Pompeii)
2. **How many people did it hold?** Sources vary slightly: "around 20,000" (Wikipedia EN), "up to 20,000" (official park site), and "20,000–24,000" (pompeiiinpictures) — roughly 20,000 is the safe figure to use, noted here as an estimate. Sources: [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheatre_of_Pompeii), [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/amphitheater/)
3. **Did people really die in the AD 59 riot?** Yes — Tacitus records deaths and injuries on both sides, severe enough that the Roman Senate banned games at Pompeii for ten years and exiled the instigators. Exact casualty numbers are not given in the sources I could access; treat any specific death toll as unverified/disputed unless sourced directly to a Tacitus translation. Source: [Wikipedia EN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheatre_of_Pompeii), [Wikipedia IT](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anfiteatro_di_Pompei)
4. **Where can I see the famous riot painting?** English Wikipedia states it is now held at the National Archaeological Museum in Naples (MANN), not on site at Pompeii. I could not confirm the specific gallery/room in the research pass — treat as "MANN, Naples" only, without a specific room number, until independently re-verified. Source: [Wikipedia EN — Amphitheatre of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheatre_of_Pompeii)

## Hebrew naming

- Amphitheatre → **אמפיתיאטרון** (transliteration: *amfite'atron*) — standard Hebrew term, used as-is by Israeli guides and in Hebrew Wikipedia/tourism material; no separate colloquial term.
- Site name, "Amphitheatre of Pompeii" → **האמפיתיאטרון של פומפיי** (*ha-amfite'atron shel Pompeii*)
- Cavea (seating tiers) → **קֶוֵאָה** (*kave'ah*) — transliterated directly, not commonly Hebraized; Israeli guides typically say "אזורי הישיבה" (*ezorei ha-yeshiva*, "seating areas") in plain description rather than using the Latin term.
- Arena → **זירה** (*zira*) — this is a native Hebrew word already in everyday use (also means "arena/ring" generally), so it needs no transliteration; guides use זירה freely.
- Hypogeum (underground substructure) → **היפוגאום** (*hipogeum*) — transliterated Latin/Greek term; less common in casual guiding, more likely explained descriptively as "המרתף התת-קרקעי" (*ha-martef ha-tat-karka'i*, "the underground basement") since this specific amphitheatre notably lacks one.
- Gladiator → **גלדיאטור** (*gladiator*) — fully assimilated loanword in Hebrew, used exactly as in English.
- Munus (gladiatorial game/show, plural munera) → **מונוס** (*munus*) / **משחקי גלדיאטורים** (*mis'chakei gladiatorim*, "gladiator games") — the Latin term itself is rarely used in Hebrew tour guiding; guides overwhelmingly prefer the descriptive Hebrew phrase "משחקי גלדיאטורים" or "קרבות גלדיאטורים" (*kravot gladiatorim*, "gladiator battles").
