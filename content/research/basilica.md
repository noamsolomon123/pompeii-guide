## What you see

1. **A large rectangular hall** — roughly 55 m long by 24 m wide (the largest roofed public building in Pompeii). Source: [it.wikipedia.org — Basilica (Pompei)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)).
2. **Tall brick-faced columns** — 28 columns arranged around the central nave (plus 4 taller Ionic columns on the façade, ~11 m), built up from wedge/triangular-cut bricks or tile segments rather than solid stone drums. Sources: [planetpompeii.com](https://www.planetpompeii.com/en/map/the-basilica.html), [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-basilica-pompei-it-65-m.htm), [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)).
3. **Faux-marble stucco finish** — walls and semi-columns were coated in "First Style" (incrustation-style) stucco, moulded and painted in panels to imitate large blocks of coloured marble (yellow, green, red), with black-and-red painted socles at the base. This is a real, well-documented Pompeian technique: stucco relief panels were shaped and painted to mimic ashlar masonry in polychrome stone, cheaper than actual marble veneer. Sources: [pompeiiinpictures.com detail page](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2001%2001%20p3.htm), [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)), [planetpompeii.com](https://www.planetpompeii.com/en/map/the-basilica.html).
4. **The raised tribunal/podium** at the west end of the hall — about 2 m high, fronted by six Corinthian columns, with no visible staircase (steps were likely a removable wooden structure). Two small side chambers flank it, possibly used for judges' consultations or holding accused persons. Sources: [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-basilica-pompei-it-65-m.htm), [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)).
5. **Graffiti covering the walls**, especially documented along the north wall under the portico — this building preserves some of Pompeii's most-cited graffiti (see History/Anecdotes below). Source: [pompeiiinpictures.com](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2001%2001%20p3.htm).
6. **The entrance vestibule/portico** on the Forum (east) side — five openings separated by tufa pillars lead from the Forum into the hall; smaller doors also open onto Via Marina on the long north and south sides. Sources: [pompeiisites.org (IT)](https://pompeiisites.org/en/pompeii-archaeological-park/basilica/), [planetpompeii.com](https://www.planetpompeii.com/en/map/the-basilica.html).
7. **A weathered, partly collapsed upper structure** — the building today survives mostly as column stumps and low walls; its upper elevation and roof are gone. Available sources document the visible stucco-faced masonry and tufa/brick construction but do **not** specifically confirm the term "opus quasi reticulatum" for this building — flagged as unverified; avoid stating that specific technique on-mic. What is confirmed: local dark volcanic stone (tufa) was used for the original 2nd-century-BC columns, with some columns later replaced in white limestone in the 1st century AD. Source: [romeartlover.it](https://www.romeartlover.it/Pompei3.html).
8. **Statue base** in the central hall (west side, near the tribunal) — believed to have held an equestrian statue. Sources: [pompeiisites.org (IT)](https://pompeiisites.org/en/pompeii-archaeological-park/basilica/), [pompeiiinpictures.com](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2001%2001%20p3.htm).

## History in layers

### (a) 30-second intro
This is the Basilica — Pompeii's law court and business exchange, and one of the oldest and largest public buildings in the entire Roman world. Built in the late 2nd century BC, it predates the grand marble Forum you just walked through by decades. Its Latin name later gave rise to the word we still use for great domed churches — though this building has nothing to do with Christianity.

### (b) 2-minute story (~150 words)
The Basilica is generally dated to around 130–120 BC, making it the oldest and largest public building surviving at Pompeii, and among the very earliest examples anywhere of the "basilica" building type that Rome would later use for law courts across its empire. It sits at the southwest corner of the Forum, built before the Forum's own later monumentalisation in stone and marble — so architecturally it belongs to an earlier phase of the city's development. Inside, three aisles were divided by two rows of tall brick-and-stucco columns, with a raised judicial tribunal at the west end. It functioned as both a courthouse and a covered marketplace/exchange for business deals — Pompeii's "stock market." Sources disagree on how the building fared in the AD 62 earthquake specifically (no dedicated repair inscription survives for it, unlike the nearby Temple of Isis), and it was standing, in use, when Vesuvius erupted in AD 79. It was first excavated in 1813, with further campaigns through 1950. Sources: [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)), [planetpompeii.com](https://www.planetpompeii.com/en/map/the-basilica.html), [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2001%2001%20p1.htm).

### (c) 2-minute deep-dive (~250 words)
Architecturally, the Pompeii Basilica matters because it is one of the earliest known buildings of the "basilica" type — a long hall with a raised platform for magistrates — a form Rome subsequently adopted for law courts and civic halls throughout the empire, and which Christian builders later borrowed wholesale for church architecture (hence the modern word "basilica" for a great church, even though this pagan building predates Christianity by centuries). Scholars broadly converge on a late-2nd-century-BC date, commonly cited as circa 130–120 BC, but the range found across sources is wider: some accounts place it as early as the early 2nd century BC and others push construction into the early 1st century BC (with a securely dated graffito — see below — proving it was in full public use by 78 BC at the latest). This spread reflects real scholarly debate over precise dating evidence versus stylistic dating. Source: [Academia.edu abstract — "Civil Forum of Pompeii: The Basilica"](https://www.academia.edu/24663347/Civil_Forum_of_Pompeii_The_Basilica) (full text was not accessible — 403 error — so only the debate's existence, not its full argumentation, could be confirmed here; flagged as thin sourcing).

The tribunal at the west end, elevated ~2 m and fronted by six Corinthian columns with no permanent stairs, physically separated magistrates from the crowd below — removable wooden steps likely controlled access.

The walls carry some of Pompeii's densest surviving graffiti, concentrated along the north portico wall, including CIL IV 1904, CIL IV 1891, and CIL IV 1837 — ranging from complaints about the sheer quantity of graffiti to love poetry with added replies from other hands, plus dated "visitor" graffiti recording named individuals and consular-year dates. These give direct evidence of who spent time here: idle professionals (lawyers, teachers, artists) waiting for clients. Source: [pompeiiinpictures.com](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2001%2001%20p3.htm). No dedicated 2020–2026 restoration or research project specific to the Basilica was found in available sources — flagged as a genuine gap, not an omission.

## The detail most visitors miss

On the **north wall of the Basilica, under the portico** (the stretch of wall pompeiiinpictures.com documents in detail near the VIII.1.2 doorway), a Roman named Gaius Pumidius Diphilus scratched his name and the exact date of his visit: *"Caius Pumidius Dipilus heic fuit ante diem V nonas octobreis Marco Lepido Quinto Catulo conselibus"* — "Gaius Pumidius Diphilus was here, five days before the Nones of October, in the consulship of Marcus Lepidus and Quintus Catulus" — a date that converts to **3 October, 78 BC**. It is one of the earliest precisely-datable graffiti in Pompeii and functions as hard proof the Basilica was already standing and in public use over 150 years before the eruption. Most visitors walk past the column stumps without noticing the wall inscriptions at all. Source: [it.wikipedia.org — Basilica (Pompei)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)).

## Anecdotes

1. **"O walls, you have held up so much tedious graffiti..."** (CIL IV 1904, found scratched on the Basilica's north wall) — a Roman visitor's own joke about the sheer volume of graffiti already covering the building in antiquity: "O walls, you have held up so much tedious graffiti that I am amazed you have not already collapsed in ruin." TRUE, sourced graffito. Source: [pompeiiinpictures.com](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2001%2001%20p3.htm) (location/CIL number); wording corroborated by [romeartlover.it](https://www.romeartlover.it/Pompei3.html)-style secondary citations of the same text across multiple sources.
2. **The dated "I was here" graffito of Gaius Pumidius Diphilus**, 3 October 78 BC (see "detail most visitors miss" above) — a real, precisely dated piece of evidence, not a legend. Source: [it.wikipedia.org — Basilica (Pompei)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)).
3. **Idle professionals killing time here**: ancient testimony/scholarly reconstruction describes the Basilica as a hangout for "lawyers without clients, teachers without pupils, artists without commissions," who whiled away slow days writing complaints on the walls — about not being invited to dinner, or about the goddess Venus not helping their love lives. This is a scholarly characterisation drawn from the graffiti corpus rather than a single ancient source, and should be presented as such (not as one specific eyewitness quote). Source: [romeartlover.it](https://www.romeartlover.it/Pompei3.html).

## FAQ

**Q: What was a Roman basilica actually for?**
A: It was a multi-purpose civic hall — part law court (with a magistrate's tribunal), part covered marketplace and business exchange where deals, loans, and contracts were negotiated, especially useful in bad weather. Source: [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-basilica-pompei-it-65-m.htm).

**Q: Is this a church?**
A: No — this building predates Christianity entirely, built roughly 130–120 BC, over a century before Christianity existed. The word "basilica" was later borrowed by Christian architects for grand churches precisely because this Roman hall-with-apse-like-tribunal layout proved so adaptable, but Pompeii's Basilica itself was a purely secular Roman law-court and business hall. Source: general architectural-history consensus reflected across [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2001%2001%20p1.htm) and [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)).

**Q: Why so many columns?**
A: The building needed to span a very large roofed hall (about 55 x 24 m) without modern structural materials, so two rows of columns — 28 in total around the central nave, built up from brick/tile segments and stuccoed — divided the space into a wide central nave and side aisles while supporting the roof. Source: [it.wikipedia.org — Basilica (Pompei)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)); [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-basilica-pompei-it-65-m.htm).

**Q: What does the graffiti actually say?**
A: It ranges widely — from a visitor's joke that the walls must be amazed they haven't collapsed under the weight of so much graffiti (CIL IV 1904), to a dated "I was here" signature from 78 BC (Gaius Pumidius Diphilus), to longer love poetry with replies added by other hands (CIL IV 1837) and complaints about not being invited to dinner. Sources: [pompeiiinpictures.com](http://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2001%2001%20p3.htm), [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_(Pompei)).
