## What you see

1. **Horseshoe-shaped cavea (auditorium)** cut into the natural hillside, Greek-style rather than the fully artificial Roman semicircle — distinguishes it from later purely Roman theatres. [pompeiitravel.com](https://pompeiitravel.com/large-theatre-pompeii-history-facts/); [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-teatro-grande-pompei-it-85-m.htm)
2. **Three seating zones (ima/media/summa cavea)** separated by corridors (praecinctiones) and low walls, visibly stepped as you look up the slope — physical evidence of Roman social stratification (elite nearest the orchestra, general public at the top). [English Wikipedia — Theatre Area of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii); [ermakvagus.com](https://ermakvagus.com/Europe/Italy/Pompeii/theatre_pompeii.html)
3. **Orchestra**, semi-circular/horseshoe performance floor at the base of the cavea, with side entrances (parodoi) via arched passages. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2007%2020%20p4.htm)
4. **Stage building (scaenae frons) remains** on the north side — the surviving stage (pulpitum) was rebuilt in stone after the AD 62 earthquake, designed to imitate the columned, niched, statue-decorated façade of a grand building; foundations and partial wall lines are visible today rather than a standing multi-story front. [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-teatro-grande-pompei-it-85-m.htm)
5. **Stone corbels/rings set into the upper cavea wall**, once holding masts for the velarium (retractable awning) that shaded spectators. [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-teatro-grande-pompei-it-85-m.htm)
6. **A carved bust of a young man with curled hair and ivy-berry clusters** (identified as a Bacchus/Dionysus image, protective deity of theatre) above the west entrance arch to the orchestra — a specific, photographed decorative detail. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2007%2020%20p4.htm)
7. **1950s iron-and-wood seating frames** installed over the original stone steps to allow modern audiences (concerts/theatre festivals) to sit without wearing down the ancient stone — a visibly modern addition distinguishable from the ancient masonry. [English Wikipedia — Theatre Area of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii)
8. **Construction material**: the cavea substructure and later Roman additions are built largely in opus incertum (irregular stone-and-mortar facing), per Italian Wikipedia and secondary sources. [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teatro_Grande_(Pompei))

**Original vs restored:** The hillside-cut lower cavea core and orchestra area are ancient (2nd century BC Hellenistic phase, remodeled Augustan-era); the visible wooden/iron bleacher framework over the steps is a 1950s modern addition for functional reuse, not ancient fabric. The theatre's original marble seat facings were largely stripped/looted in antiquity or later centuries per pompeii.org.uk ("original marble seating was removed and carried off after the destruction of the town"), so most bare stone visible today is the structural core, not the ancient marble finish. [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-teatro-grande-pompei-it-85-m.htm); [English Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii)

**MANN Naples:** No specific object from the Large Theatre itself (e.g., sculpture, seat inscription) was confirmed as relocated to MANN in the sources checked in this research pass. Finds directly tied to MANN in this research were from the adjacent **gladiator barracks/Quadriporticus of the Theatres** (see History in layers, below), not the theatre auditorium proper — this distinction should be kept in the narration. No MANN object list for the theatre itself could be verified; flagged as unconfirmed rather than stated as fact.

## History in layers

### (a) 30-second intro
Cut into a natural hillside in the 2nd century BC, the Large Theatre could seat around 5,000 people and staged the comedies and tragedies of Greek-Roman tradition. Wealthy patrons rebuilt it in fine Roman style under Emperor Augustus, and it still hosts live performances today — one of very few ancient theatres in the world in continuous, if intermittent, use. [English Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii); [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/monuments/large-theatre/)

### (b) ~150 words
The Large Theatre's Hellenistic-style core was built around the middle of the 2nd century BC, exploiting the natural slope of the hill for its tiered seating — one of the first permanent stone theatres anywhere in the Roman world. [English Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii) It was substantially renovated in the Augustan period (traditionally dated around 2 BC), a project inscriptions credit both to the architect **Marcus Artorius Primus**, who reworked the stage, added a velarium awning, and numbered the seats, and to patrons **M. Holconius Rufus and M. Holconius Celer**, who funded the crypta (covered corridor), tribunalia (VIP boxes) and cavea "at their own expense" per the inscription "M.M. Holconii Rufus et Celer criptam, tribunalia, theatrum sua pecunia." [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/monuments/large-theatre/); [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teatro_Grande_(Pompei)) It held roughly 5,000 spectators. [English Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii) The AD 62 earthquake damaged the stage, which was rebuilt in stone; the theatre was then buried by the AD 79 eruption and excavated in stages from 1764 onward. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/monuments/large-theatre/); [pompeiitravel.com](https://pompeiitravel.com/large-theatre-pompeii-history-facts/)

### (c) ~250-word deep-dive
Scholarship generally separates the theatre's building history into at least three phases: an original Samnite-period predecessor later rebuilt in Hellenistic style by the mid-2nd century BC; a further Roman remodeling around 80–70 BC after Pompeii became a Roman colony; and the major Augustan-era rebuild (~2 BC) financed by the Holconius family and overseen by architect Marcus Artorius Primus, whose name appears in a dedicatory inscription alongside details of the specific work done — stage modifications, the velarium, and numbered seating. [pompeiitravel.com](https://pompeiitravel.com/large-theatre-pompeii-history-facts/); [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/monuments/large-theatre/) Seating hierarchy is directly attested: the ima cavea (lowest, nearest the orchestra) was reserved for decurions/nobility, with sources noting a specific honorary seat for Marcus Holconius Rufus marked "in bronze letters"; the media cavea served guilds and the mercantile middle class; the summa cavea, the smallest and steepest section, held ordinary townspeople. [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-teatro-grande-pompei-it-85-m.htm); [ermakvagus.com](https://ermakvagus.com/Europe/Italy/Pompeii/theatre_pompeii.html) The theatre connects directly to the adjacent Quadriporticus of the Theatres (originally a covered portico for spectators to shelter or promenade between acts); after the AD 62 earthquake this portico was repurposed as a gladiator barracks and training ground ("caserma dei gladiatori"), a conversion documented in both English and Italian sources. [English Wikipedia — Theatre Area of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii) Excavation of the theatre proceeded in campaigns across 1764–1765, 1767–1769, 1773, 1789, 1791–1794, 1902, and 1951. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/monuments/large-theatre/) No verified source in this research pass documented WWII bomb damage specifically to the Large Theatre building (Pompeii as a whole was hit by Allied bombing raids in 1943, per general Wikipedia text, but that source did not name the theatre specifically) — flagged as unconfirmed. [English Wikipedia — Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii) No dedicated 2020–2026 restoration-study press release specific to the Large Theatre auditorium was located via pompeiisites.org in this pass, beyond its continued active use for the annual "Pompeii Theatrum Mundi" festival and 2026's "Serate nella Grande Pompei" evening performance series (Aug 28–Oct 10, 2026). [pompeiisites.org events](https://pompeiisites.org/en/events/pompeii-theatrum-mundi/)

## The detail most visitors miss

Look above the **west-side arched entrance into the orchestra** (the parodos/passage on the west of the cavea): a small carved bust of a curly-haired young man wreathed in ivy berries is set into the stonework — identified as Bacchus/Dionysus, the patron deity of theatre, placed there as a protective figure over performers and audience. Most visitors walk straight past it looking at the seating instead. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2007%2020%20p4.htm)

## Anecdotes

1. **The Holconius honorary seat.** An inscription in bronze letters marked a specific reserved seat for Marcus Holconius Rufus, one of the theatre's chief patrons and a five-time duovir (city magistrate) of Pompeii — a rare, concrete physical trace of a named individual's civic status literally carved into the architecture. Sourced (not legend). [pompeii.org.uk](https://www.pompeii.org.uk/m.php/museo-teatro-grande-pompei-it-85-m.htm)
2. **Still a working stage.** Modern performers — including, per pompeiisites.org's own event listings, Italian singer-songwriters such as Daniele Silvestri and Biagio Antonacci — have performed concerts at the Large Theatre in recent years, making it one of the few ancient Roman theatres anywhere still used for its original purpose (live public performance) roughly 2,000 years after it was built. Sourced. [pompeiisites.org event listings](https://pompeiisites.org/event/daniele-silvestri-teatro-grande/), [https://pompeiisites.org/event/biagio-antonacci-teatro-grande/]
3. **Gladiators moved in after the quake.** Following the AD 62 earthquake, the once-elegant portico behind the theatre (the Quadriporticus, meant for strolling theatregoers) was converted into cramped gladiator barracks and training rooms — a documented downgrade in the area's function in the years just before the eruption. Sourced. [English Wikipedia — Theatre Area of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii)

## FAQ

1. **Can I sit in the seats?** During the day the ancient stone steps are generally viewed rather than sat on for regular touring; note that a portion of the seating in recent years has had modern removable/iron-framed seating installed for scheduled evening performances rather than for casual daytime seating. [pompeiitravel.com](https://pompeiitravel.com/large-theatre-pompeii-history-facts/); [English Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii)
2. **How many people did it hold?** Ancient capacity is generally given as approximately 5,000 spectators; one secondary source (pompeiitravel.com) separately cites a reduced modern operating capacity of about 2,200 for present-day events, though this modern figure was not corroborated on pompeiisites.org itself. [English Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii); [pompeiitravel.com](https://pompeiitravel.com/large-theatre-pompeii-history-facts/)
3. **Why was some of the theatre closed in September/October 2026?** Per pompeiisites.org's own visitor notice, from September 9 to October 8, 2026 the Large Theatre was "visible from above from the upper ring, with access from the Triangular Forum" only — i.e., visitors could look down into it from the top rather than enter the lower cavea/orchestra directly, most likely tied to the site's evening performance series running in the same period. This tour's October 20, 2026 date falls after that stated window. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/houses/)
4. **Is the theatre connected to other ruins nearby?** Yes — it directly adjoins the Quadriporticus of the Theatres (used first as a portico for spectators, later as gladiator barracks after AD 62) and sits near the Temple of Isis and the smaller covered Odeon (built c. 80 BC, capacity ~1,500), together forming Pompeii's "theatre district." [English Wikipedia — Theatre Area of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii)

## Hebrew naming

- Large Theatre / Teatro Grande — **התיאטרון הגדול** (ha-te'atron ha-gadol); transliteration of the Italian "Teatro Grande" as a proper name: **טאטרו גרנדה** (used less commonly by guides than the descriptive Hebrew "התיאטרון הגדול").
- **Cavea** (seating bowl) — **קָוֵאָה** (kave'ah); commonly explained in Hebrew guiding simply as "אזור הישיבה המדורג" (the tiered seating area) rather than transliterated.
- **Scaenae frons** (ornamental stage backdrop wall) — **סקנה פרונס** / described descriptively as "קיר הבמה המעוטר" (the decorated stage wall).
- **Orchestra** (performance floor at the base of the cavea) — **אורקסטרה** (orkestrah) — same word as in modern Hebrew, easily recognizable to a Hebrew-speaking audience, though its ancient meaning (dance/performance floor) differs from the modern "orchestra" (musicians).
- **Vomitorium** (a vaulted entrance/exit passage for crowds) — **וומיטוריום** (vomitoryum); guides often gloss it directly since it has no natural Hebrew equivalent, sometimes explaining it as "פתח יציאה מהיר להמונים."
- **Velarium** (retractable awning for shade) — **וולריום** (velaryum), explained descriptively as "יריעת בד להצללה."

*(Note: no authoritative published Hebrew-language guiding glossary for Pompeii's theatre was located in this research pass; the transliterations above follow standard Hebrew phonetic transliteration conventions for Latin loanwords, not a verified Israeli-guide-industry source — flagged as best-effort, not sourced to a guide-specific reference.)*
