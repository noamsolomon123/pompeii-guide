# House of the Tragic Poet (Casa del Poeta Tragico) — id 8, Regio VI

## Identity

- **Official name:** House of the Tragic Poet (English); **Casa del Poeta Tragico** (Italian, the name used on-site and by the Parco Archeologico di Pompei). [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2003.htm)
- **Address / catalog numbers:** Regio VI, Insula 8. The house is a single linked property with **two doorways, entrance 3 and entrance 5** (catalogued as VI.8.3 and VI.8.5, often written together as **VI.8.3-5**). Entrance 3 (with the Cave Canem mosaic) is the main/public entrance; entrance 5 is a secondary doorway into the same house. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VI.8.3](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2003.htm) · [pompeiiinpictures.com — VI.8.5 plan](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2005%20plan.htm) · [Italian Wikipedia gives "VI 8,3"](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_del_Poeta_Tragico) · [Wikidata gives "VI.8.5"](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q4177608) (the two figures both refer to doorways of the same connected building — sources are not contradictory, just citing different doorways).

## What you see

1. **The CAVE CANEM mosaic** at the threshold of the main entrance (VI.8.3): a black-and-white mosaic of a growling, chained guard dog with the inscribed warning **"CAVE CANEM"** ("beware of the dog") beneath it, set into the floor of the fauces (entrance corridor). [Wikipedia — House of the Tragic Poet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_the_Tragic_Poet) · [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2005.htm)
2. **Important: the mosaic seen in situ today is a replica.** The **original Cave Canem mosaic was removed and is held at the Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli (MANN)**; a **faithful copy/replica is set in the original doorway at Pompeii**, now protected behind a glass panel/window at the entrance. [pompeiicity.com](https://pompeiicity.com/en/places/cave-canem) — corroborated by the pattern of removals from this house generally (see below).
3. **Tuscan atrium with impluvium** — the rainwater catch-basin at the centre of the atrium is still visible, a standard feature of the atrium-house layout. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VI.8.3](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2003%20p4.htm)
4. **Peristyle garden** with a colonnaded portico, at the rear of the house, opening off the tablinum; a small **lararium** (household shrine, an aedicula/niche form) is set against its north wall. [Madain-derived plan data via pompeiiinpictures — VI.8.3](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2003.htm)
5. **The theatrical/"tragic poet" mosaic emblema** that gave the house its name — a mosaic panel from the tablinum floor showing an actor (or actors) preparing backstage with theatrical masks and costume, now removed to MANN (inventory number **9986**); only the surrounding floor pattern remains in situ, the central emblema is a void today. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VI.8.3](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2003%20p4.htm)
6. **Fourth-style wall paintings** in several rooms (red/yellow/black zoccolo-and-panel schemes) — e.g. in the corridor near the entrance and in room 14, though many figured "megalographic" panels (large mythological scenes) were cut out and removed to Naples, leaving mostly the decorative framing in situ. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2005%20p5.htm) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_the_Tragic_Poet)
7. **An electoral graffito** scratched onto the right-hand pilaster of the entrance: "M(arcum) Holconium / C(aium) Gavium aed(iles)" (CIL IV 262) — an election endorsement for two men running for the office of aedile, typical of the political graffiti found across Pompeii's streets and doorways. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2005.htm)
8. **Kitchen with hearth and latrine**, and a plaster impression of a stairway to a now-lost upper floor, visible in one of the rear service rooms — concrete evidence the house had a second storey. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VI.8.3](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2003%20p4.htm)

**In situ vs MANN — summary:** The visible Cave Canem mosaic at the door is a **replica** (original in MANN). The tablinum's theatrical mosaic emblema (inv. 9986), the marble faun statuette from the tablinum (inv. 6347), and major figured frescoes — including the *Sacrifice of Iphigenia* (inv. 9112, peristyle north wall), *Achilles and Briseis*, and the *Nuptials/Hierogamy of Zeus and Hera* — were all detached and are now displayed at MANN. Decorative wall zoccolos, the impluvium/atrium structure, the peristyle colonnade, and the lararium remain in situ (empty of their finest figural panels). [pompeiiinpictures.com — VI.8.3](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2003%20p4.htm) · [it.wikipedia.org — Sacrificio di Ifigenia (Pompei)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrificio_di_Ifigenia_%28Pompei%29) · [it.wikipedia.org — Casa del Poeta Tragico](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_del_Poeta_Tragico)

## History in layers

**(a) 30-second intro (3 sentences):**
Behind an ordinary-looking doorway in Regio VI sits a house famous the world over for one small mosaic: a chained dog and the words CAVE CANEM, "beware of the dog." Built in the 2nd century BCE and likely redecorated after the earthquake of AD 62, the house was buried — along with the rest of Pompeii — by Vesuvius in AD 79. Its real name comes from a mosaic of actors preparing for a play, found in the main reception room, which early excavators mistook for a poet reading his tragic verses.

**(b) 2-minute story (~150 words):**
Excavated in November 1824–1825 under archaeologist Antonio Bonucci, the House of the Tragic Poet became one of the most celebrated houses in Pompeii almost immediately, thanks to its rich mythological frescoes and the vivid Cave Canem mosaic at its threshold. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2003.htm) Ten years later it achieved lasting fame in fiction: English author Edward Bulwer-Lytton, who had studied William Gell's detailed publication *Pompeiana*, used this house as the model for the home of his hero Glaucus in the bestselling 1834 novel *The Last Days of Pompeii*. [Victorianweb.org / summarized via search of scholarship on Bulwer-Lytton and Gell's Pompeiana](http://www.victorianweb.org/authors/bulwer/pompeii/photos/2.html) Structurally, the house was originally built in the 2nd century BCE and, per Italian-language scholarship, was likely completely redecorated after the AD 62 earthquake — meaning most of what was found in 1824 reflects that late, pre-eruption remodel rather than the house's original 2nd-century BCE appearance. [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_del_Poeta_Tragico)

**(c) ~250-word deep-dive:**
The identity of the owner is unresolved. Italian scholarship notes a long-standing puzzle: the house is modest in footprint compared with grand Pompeian domus like the House of the Faun, yet its decorative program (multiple large mythological megalographies, a marble faun statuette, fine floor mosaics) is of very high quality — a mismatch that has fueled debate over whether the owner was a wealthy individual of relatively modest social rank investing heavily in cultural display, rather than an old aristocratic family. [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_del_Poeta_Tragico) No inscription definitively names an owner.

Stylistically, the surviving and recorded wall decoration is attributed to the **Fourth Pompeian Style** (the last decorative phase before AD 79), consistent with the post-62-earthquake redecoration. [en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_the_Tragic_Poet) · [it.wikipedia.org — Sacrificio di Ifigenia](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrificio_di_Ifigenia_%28Pompei%29) The house's very name is itself the product of a 19th-century misreading: early excavators and William Gell described a fresco figure as a "tragic poet" reciting verses; that panel is now identified by scholars as a scene of **Admetus and Alcestis** consulting the oracle, from Euripides' tragedy — a mythological, not literary-biographical, subject. The name stuck regardless and is still used today. [Wikimedia Commons file description / cross-referenced scholarship](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Admetus_and_Alcestis_fresco_from_House_of_the_Tragic_Poet_(Pompeii).jpg)

An electoral graffito for two candidates for aedile (CIL IV 262) survives scratched on the entrance pilaster, typical of Pompeii's ubiquitous street-level political campaigning. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2005.htm) No major new excavation has taken place at this already-excavated house in 2020–2026; the notable recent activity is **conservation work on the Cave Canem mosaic and its protective glass display at the entrance**, intended to keep the mosaic visible to visitors while shielding it from wear — exact restoration dates were not confirmed in official Parco Archeologico publications found during this research and should be verified with park staff if needed for the script. [pompeiicity.com — Cave Canem](https://pompeiicity.com/en/places/cave-canem)

## The detail most visitors miss

Most visitors stop at the Cave Canem mosaic by the door and never look past it. But push on (access permitting) to the **peristyle garden at the rear**, where excavators in 1825 recovered the impression/remains of a **tortoise shell** among the garden soil — physical evidence that the household likely kept a pet tortoise in the garden, a small domestic detail easy to miss amid the house's famous mythological artwork. Look for the **lararium niche on the peristyle's north wall** while you're back there — it's the household shrine, often walked past without a glance. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VI.8.3](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R6/6%2008%2003.htm)

## Anecdotes

1. **True, sourced:** The house you see today at the entrance shows a *replica* of the Cave Canem mosaic, protected behind glass — the original was removed to Naples generations ago. Visitors photographing "the ancient mosaic" at the doorway are in fact photographing a modern copy laid in the original spot. [pompeiicity.com](https://pompeiicity.com/en/places/cave-canem)
2. **True, sourced:** The house's name is based on a 19th-century mistake. Early excavators, following William Gell's description, believed a fresco (now in Naples) showed a "tragic poet" reading his own verses to an audience. Scholars have since identified the same scene as the mythological couple Admetus and Alcestis consulting an oracle — a completely different subject — but by then the name "House of the Tragic Poet" had already stuck and was never changed. [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Admetus_and_Alcestis_fresco_from_House_of_the_Tragic_Poet_(Pompeii).jpg)
3. **True, sourced, literary connection (flagged as an influence, not a documentary depiction):** Victorian novelist Edward Bulwer-Lytton used this house — via William Gell's illustrated study *Pompeiana* — as the architectural model for the home of his hero Glaucus in *The Last Days of Pompeii* (1834), one of the 19th century's most popular novels and a major reason this specific house became internationally famous well beyond archaeological circles. This is a documented literary influence; the novel itself is fiction and its plot events did not occur at this house. [Victorianweb.org, house photo captioned for Bulwer-Lytton's novel](http://www.victorianweb.org/authors/bulwer/pompeii/photos/2.html)

## FAQ

1. **Is the Cave Canem mosaic at the door the real ancient one?** No — the mosaic you see in the entrance today is a replica set in the original spot and protected by glass; the original ancient mosaic is preserved at the Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli (MANN). [pompeiicity.com](https://pompeiicity.com/en/places/cave-canem)
2. **Why is it called the "House of the Tragic Poet" if there's no poet mentioned anywhere?** The name comes from a 19th-century misidentification of a fresco (now in MANN) as a poet reciting tragic verses; it actually depicts the mythological figures Admetus and Alcestis. The name predates the correction and was never updated. [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Admetus_and_Alcestis_fresco_from_House_of_the_Tragic_Poet_(Pompeii).jpg)
3. **Do I need a special ticket to enter?** No — it is included in the standard general Pompeii site ticket (currently €20 as of the January 2026 tariff update); no separate booking is required beyond normal park entry. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/visiting-info/timetables-and-tickets/)
4. **Is the house always open?** It is currently listed as open on the official Parco Archeologico "open buildings" page, but this house has a documented history of periodic access restrictions to protect its mosaic and frescoes, so guides/visitors should re-check shortly before visiting. [pompeiisites.org — Open buildings](https://pompeiisites.org/en/houses/)

## Hebrew naming

- House of the Tragic Poet / Casa del Poeta Tragico → **בית המשורר הטרגי** (Beit ha-Meshorer ha-Tragi) — this is the natural, commonly used Hebrew rendering among Israeli tour guides and Hebrew-language travel writing on Pompeii; some guides also say **"בית המשורר הטראגי"** (with an extra א, closer to the English "tragic"), both are understood.
- Cave Canem → transliterate as **"קווה קאנם"** (keve kanem) when quoting the Latin, and gloss it in Hebrew as **"היזהר מהכלב"** ("beware of the dog") — guides typically say the Latin phrase first, then translate.
- Mosaic → **פסיפס** (pesifas) — standard, universally used term.
- Fresco / wall painting → **פרסקו** (fresko) or **ציור קיר** (tziyur kir).
- Atrium → **אטריום** (atrium) — used as-is, no separate Hebrew word.
- Impluvium (the rainwater basin in the atrium) → **אימפלוביום** (impluvium), sometimes explained simply as **"בריכת איסוף מי גשמים"** (rainwater collection pool).
- Peristyle (colonnaded garden courtyard) → **פריסטיל** or **פריסטיליון** (peristil / peristilion).
- Tablinum (reception/office room facing the atrium) → **טבלינום** (tablinum).
- Triclinium (formal dining room) → **טריקליניום** (triclinium).
- Lararium (household shrine) → **לררium** is awkward in Hebrew script; guides typically say **"מזבח בית"** (mizbe'ach bayit, "household altar") or transliterate **לרריום** (lararium).
- Emblema (the fine central mosaic panel set into a floor) → **אמבלמה** (emblema) or simply described as "הפסיפס המרכזי" (the central mosaic).
