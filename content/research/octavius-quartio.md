# House of Octavius Quartio (House of Loreius Tiburtinus) — II.2.2

## Identity

**Official name (EN):** House of Octavius Quartio. **Popular/traditional name:** House of Loreius Tiburtinus. **Official name (IT):** Casa di Octavius Quartio (also widely called *Casa di Loreio Tiburtino*).

**The naming confusion, explained:** For most of the 20th century the house was known only as the "House of Loreius Tiburtinus." Wikipedia states the naming "was wrongly derived from electoral graffiti etched in the outer façade, some saying 'Vote for Loreius' and others 'Vote for Tiburtinus'" — i.e., early excavators assumed the candidates named in campaign notices (*programmata*) painted on the street-facing wall were the house's owners [en.wikipedia.org]. This was a common 19th/early-20th-century excavation error at Pompeii (electoral graffiti were painted by supporters on any convenient wall, not necessarily the candidate's own house). The true identity of the owner was established later from an artifact found inside during excavation: "the last known owner of the house was a man named Octavius Quartio, whose bronze seal [signet ring] was found inside the house during excavation" [en.wikipedia.org]. The Pompeii Parco Archeologico's own site gives his full name as **Decimus Octavius Quartio**, and notes he was a member of the *Augustales* — "civilians dedicated to the cult of the emperors" [pompeiisites.org]. Both names remain in use: "Octavius Quartio" is now the scholarly/official designation, while "Loreius Tiburtinus" persists in older guidebooks, older site signage, and casual usage. Note: an inscription inside the house (room f) reads "AMISUSIUS LOREIUS TIBURTINUS" (CIL IV 7534) — this is very likely the source of the "Loreius Tiburtinus" graffiti/name, but pompeiiinpictures.com notes the inscription itself "is no longer readable" today and is known only from earlier scholarly documentation (Warscher, 1930) [pompeiiinpictures.com].

**Regio/Insula/Address:** Regio II, Insula 2, entrance 2 (full property sometimes given as II, 2, 2–5), on the south side of the eastern stretch of Via dell'Abbondanza, near the Amphitheatre and Palaestra [en.wikipedia.org].

## What you see

1. **The euripus (canal) system** — two perpendicular artificial water channels, named after the Euripus Strait: an "upper euripus" (room *i*, running roughly east–west) and a much longer "lower euripus" running north–south, which bisects the main garden below the pseudoperistyle [pompeiiinpictures.com; en.wikipedia.org].
2. **The summer biclinium (room *k*)** — two masonry dining couches flanking a rectangular water basin/channel at the head of the lower euripus, designed for outdoor summer dining beside running water; includes a marble kneeling satyr telamon and a river-god statuette by the water [pompeiiinpictures.com].
3. **Mythological frescoes in the biclinium** — on the east wall: Narcissus admiring his reflection (north-east corner) and the tragic story of Pyramus and Thisbe, framed by a painted aedicula with Corinthian columns; still **in situ**, though faded [pompeiiinpictures.com; en.wikipedia.org].
4. **The only known Pompeian painter's signature** — "Lucius pinxit" ("Lucius painted this"), once inscribed on the red plaster of the biclinium's south couch (CIL IV 7535); the physical inscription is "no longer visible" today [pompeiiinpictures.com].
5. **Triclinium (room *h*)** with two fresco friezes, **in situ**: an upper band with eight episodes from the life of Heracles, and a lower ~30 cm-high frieze of Iliad/Trojan War scenes (Achilles dragging Hector's body behind his chariot; the funeral games of Patroclus; Priam ransoming Hector's body) [pompeiiinpictures.com].
6. **Room *f*, the Egyptian/Isis-themed oecus** — Fourth Style decoration including a seated Bacchus with thyrsus, a priest of Isis, and portrait medallions; damaged by Allied bombing on 19 September 1943 (the painted stucco fell and was "largely saved and restored from numerous small fragments") [pompeiiinpictures.com].
7. **The nymphaeum/aedicula shrine** at the junction of the upper and lower euripi — a small temple-like structure with marble cascade steps and traces of painted decoration on its walls, including a scene of Diana (Artemis) and Actaeon [pompeiiinpictures.com].
8. **The reconstructed garden planting** — rows of fruit trees and acanthus in a pergolated layout, based on archaeobotanical (root-cavity) evidence rather than guesswork [en.wikipedia.org].

**On site vs. MANN Naples:** Nearly all documented fresco cycles in this house (biclinium, triclinium, room f) remain **in situ**, unusually well-preserved for Pompeii — pompeiiinpictures.com's room-by-room pages consistently describe them as present in place, only noting the WWII stucco loss/restoration in room f [pompeiiinpictures.com]. The one confirmed object removed to storage/display elsewhere is a small white marble **cupid-with-a-mask statuette**, found above the nymphaeum's marble steps in 1920 (inventory SAP 20513), now displayed in the on-site Palaestra exhibition rather than in the house itself [pompeiiinpictures.com]. No fresco panel from this house was specifically confirmed as relocated to MANN Naples in the sources checked; flag this as **unconfirmed either way** for MANN holdings — a museum-inventory search would be needed to state this definitively.

## History in layers

**(a) 30-second intro:** This is one of Pompeii's most romantic houses — not for its size, but for its water. A long ornamental canal, the euripus, runs the length of a garden that once held real fruit trees and vines. It was built to feel like a countryside villa, shrunk down and squeezed into a city block, by a man whose actual name (Octavius Quartio) was only rediscovered because he dropped his signet ring inside.

**(b) ~150 words:** The house was likely owned by Decimus Octavius Quartio, a member of the *Augustales* — the college of freedmen devoted to the imperial cult — as identified by his bronze seal ring found during excavation [pompeiisites.org; en.wikipedia.org]. The house began in the Samnite period (roughly 3rd century BC) as a more modest dwelling, but its signature feature — the long euripus canal and the grand pergolated garden — belongs to a major renovation completed in the years after the AD 62 earthquake, in the final building phase before the eruption [en.wikipedia.org]. The garden's ancient planting scheme (rows of fruit trees and acanthus) is known not from written sources but from root cavities left in the volcanic fill, cast and studied using techniques pioneered by archaeobotanist Wilhelmina Jashemski, whose Pompeii fieldwork ran from 1961 to 1984 [en.wikipedia.org]. Both the AD 62 earthquake and the AD 79 eruption reshaped the property: post-earthquake, part of the house was subdivided and sold off before the surviving portion was rebuilt with its famous garden [en.wikipedia.org]. Systematic excavation began in 1916.

**(c) ~250-word deep-dive:** The house's fresco program is a deliberate mythological narrative built around water and its dangers/pleasures: Narcissus drowning in self-love at a fountain, the doomed lovers Pyramus and Thisbe, and — at the nymphaeum — Diana surprised bathing by the hunter Actaeon, who is destroyed by his own hounds; the triclinium adds a heroic register with Heracles' labors and the Trojan War's darkest scenes (Hector's corpse dragged behind Achilles' chariot) [pompeiiinpictures.com]. Scholars read this as a program about mortality, desire, and the perils of trespassing into forbidden or sacred space — apt for an owner using myth to dress up new-money status in old aristocratic imagery. The garden itself is a landmark of archaeobotany: root cavities preserved in the AD 79 ash/pumice fill allowed 20th-century excavators to identify the species and planting pattern of the ancient garden (fruit trees, acanthus, and shade plants in ordered, pergolated rows), a technique associated with Wilhelmina Jashemski's broader Pompeii garden studies (1961–1984) [en.wikipedia.org] — though the sources checked for this file do not confirm the exact year her team worked this specific garden, so that detail should be verified further before quoting a precise date on-air. The owner's exact social status is debated: the Augustales were wealthy freedmen (former slaves) rather than freeborn elites, and the house's mythological "high culture" decoration is sometimes read by scholars as social aspiration. Room f was damaged in the Allied bombing of Pompeii on **19 September 1943**; painted stucco fell and had to be reassembled from fragments [pompeiiinpictures.com]. No specific 2020–2026 restoration campaign for this house was found in the sources checked (flagged as thin/unconfirmed below), beyond the August 2026 reopening-to-visitors announcement.

## The detail most visitors miss

In room *f* (the Egyptian-themed oecus, on the west side of the pseudoperistyle), look for the faint remains of an inscription beneath the painted figure of the priest of Isis on the south wall. It reads "AMISUSIUS LOREIUS TIBURTINUS" (catalogued as CIL IV 7534) — almost certainly the actual origin of the house's old popular name, separate from the exterior electoral graffiti. The inscription itself is no longer legible today and is known only from early-20th-century scholarly recording (Warscher, 1930), so what you're looking at is the faded ghost of a name that shaped how this house was known for a century [pompeiiinpictures.com].

## Anecdotes

1. **The vanished bronze signet ring.** The house's true owner was unknown for decades after excavation began — visitors and even scholars called it "Loreius Tiburtinus" based on election slogans on the outside wall. It was only the discovery of a bronze signet ring inside, engraved for Octavius Quartio, that let archaeologists correct the record [en.wikipedia.org]. This is a documented fact, not legend, per Wikipedia's sourcing.
2. **A painter who signed his work.** Almost no Pompeian wall-painting is signed — painters were tradesmen, not credited artists. In the summer biclinium of this house, one exception was recorded: "Lucius pinxit," painted directly onto the plaster of the dining couch. The physical inscription itself has since faded/vanished, but it was documented and catalogued as CIL IV 7535 [pompeiiinpictures.com]. This is sourced fact, and worth flagging on-air as one of the rare named "artist credits" from the whole city.
3. **War damage, three days before Naples fell.** On 19 September 1943, Allied bombing struck the archaeological area and damaged the Egyptian-themed room (f); painted stucco fell from the walls. Restorers were later able to reassemble much of it from the fallen fragments [pompeiiinpictures.com]. This is a sourced fact from pompeiiinpictures.com's photographic documentation notes, useful for grounding the site's more recent, non-ancient history.

## FAQ

**Q: Why do some guidebooks call this the "House of Loreius Tiburtinus" and others "House of Octavius Quartio"?** Both names refer to the same house. "Loreius Tiburtinus" was an early, mistaken name based on election campaign graffiti found outside; "Octavius Quartio" is the name of the actual owner, established from a signet ring found inside during excavation [en.wikipedia.org]. Modern scholarship and the park's own site use "Octavius Quartio," but the older name is still common in guides and older signage.

**Q: Was the long canal (euripus) actually filled with flowing water in antiquity?** Yes — the euripus was fed by a lead-piped water supply (a *castellum plumbeum*/reservoir system is described for this property) and functioned as a garden water feature with fountains and cascades, not a purely decorative dry trough [en.wikipedia.org].

**Q: Are the frescoes here original, or reconstructions?** The great majority of the documented frescoes (biclinium, triclinium, room f) are original ancient paintings still in their original location (in situ), which is unusually good preservation for Pompeii; the main exception is the WWII-damaged stucco in room f, which was physically reassembled/restored from surviving fragments after 1943 bomb damage [pompeiiinpictures.com]. The garden planting you see is a modern archaeological reconstruction based on root-cavity evidence, not original ancient vegetation.

**Q: Who actually lived here?** Decimus Octavius Quartio, understood to have been a member of the *Augustales* — the board of (typically freedman) civic officials devoted to the cult of the emperor — based on the signet ring evidence, per the park's official description [pompeiisites.org].

## Hebrew naming

- **House of Octavius Quartio** — בֵּית אוֹקְטָבְיוּס קְוַארְטְיוֹ (transliteration: *Beit Oktavyus Kvartyo*). This is the scholarly/current official name.
- **House of Loreius Tiburtinus** — בֵּית לוֹרֵאוּס טִיבּוּרְטִינוּס (transliteration: *Beit Loreus Tiburtinus*). This is the older/popular name still seen on many maps, signs, and in older guide material; Israeli tour guides may still default to this name since it is the one printed on many site maps and older Hebrew-language guidebooks.
- **Euripus** (canal/water channel) — אֶאוּרִיפּוּס / תְּעָלַת מַיִם (transliteration: *Eyripus* / *te'alat mayim* — "water channel," the descriptive Hebrew term often used by guides instead of the Latin loanword).
- **Triclinium** (formal three-couch dining room) — טְרִיקְלִינְיוּם (transliteration: *Triklinyum*); often glossed in Hebrew simply as "חדר האוכל" (dining room).
- **Biclinium** (two-couch outdoor dining set, as in this house's summer dining area) — בִּיקְלִינְיוּם (transliteration: *Biklinyum*) — less commonly known, worth explaining on the spot rather than assuming the term is familiar.
- **Aedicula** (small shrine/niche, often architecturally framed like a miniature temple) — אֶדִיקוּלָה (transliteration: *Edikula*); commonly glossed in Hebrew as "גומחת פולחן קטנה" (small worship niche) or "מקדש זעיר" (tiny shrine).
- **Peristylium/peristyle** (colonnaded garden courtyard) — פֶּרִיסְטִילְיוּם / פֶּרִיסְטִיל (transliteration: *Peristilyum* / *Peristil*). Note for the script: this house technically has a **pseudoperistyle** (a partial/irregular colonnade, not a full one) according to the room labels used by pompeiiinpictures.com — worth a precise note if a guide wants to use the term accurately rather than loosely.
