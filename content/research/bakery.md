## What you see

1. **Four (originally) lava-stone hourglass mills (molae)** standing in a row in the milling yard, built from Orvieto basalt — pompeiiinpictures.com's detailed page notes "five millstones currently visible in the bakery, only four truly in place," with one stone displaced (likely between the 1970s and 2000s) and resting on modern fill. In situ originals. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)
2. **Carved letter-marks on the millstones** — quarry/carrier marks, not bakers' marks: up to "sixteen different names, possibly reducible to fourteen," each abbreviated to about three letters, most likely identifying the quarry or transporter of the stone rather than the bakery's owner. In situ. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)
3. **Basalt paving blocks arranged around the mills**, forming the working floor of the milling yard. In situ. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)
4. **The masonry oven (furnus)** in the eastern part of the bakery, with both its north and south walls and its interior chamber preserved — the same low-domed, arch-mouthed design routinely described as the direct ancestor of the modern wood-fired Neapolitan pizza oven. In situ original. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022.htm)
5. **A large kneading/mixing trough**, whose wooden scraping blade — intact when documented in 1886 — survives today only in a badly damaged state, reduced to under 20 cm. In situ, but heavily deteriorated since 19th-century documentation. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)
6. **A "bread pass" opening** connecting a northern room — interpreted as the space where shaped loaves were prepared — directly through to the oven, for moving raw loaves to the fire. In situ. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)
7. **A rectangular basin in the peristyle area south of the oven**, interpreted as a grain-moistening tank (wetting grain before grinding was a known step at other Pompeian bakeries too). In situ. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)
8. **A part-excavated cellar in front of the oven**, still partly filled with eruption material, about 1.6 m deep, built of basalt-and-limestone rubble in mortar, with nail and wood-fragment evidence suggesting a wooden floor once covered its southern half. In situ, partly unexcavated. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)

No objects from this specific address (VII.2.22) were identified in the sources checked as being on display at MANN Naples; the famous carbonized bread finds described below (Anecdotes) come from other Pompeii/Herculaneum bakeries, not from this one.

## History in layers

**(a) 30-second intro:**
This is a Roman pistrinum — a combined flour mill and bakery — one of Pompeii's roughly 30-plus such businesses. Four lava-stone hourglass mills once stood in a row beside a brick oven shaped like today's pizza ovens. Site documentation names it the Bakery of Popidius Priscus, an attribution shared by two independent modern sources for this exact address.

**(b) 2-minute story (~150 words):**
Bread was Pompeii's staple food, and a pistrinum like this one was a full production chain under one roof: grain was delivered, ground on hourglass-shaped stone mills, kneaded into dough, shaped, and baked in a wood-fired brick oven — often sold straight to the street from an attached counter, as was standard at Pompeii's bakeries. This bakery sits in Regio VII, Insula 2, opening onto the ancient lane pompeiiinpictures.com records as "Vicolo Storto." It was excavated across three separate 19th-century campaigns — 1834, 1844, and 1865 — as Bourbon- and then Italian-era archaeologists slowly cleared this part of the old town. Whether the bakery was still fully operational in its final years, and what damage (if any) it sustained in the AD 62 earthquake that struck the town 17 years before Vesuvius erupted, was not documented in the sources checked for this stop. [pompeiiinpictures.com](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022.htm)

**(c) Deep-dive (~250 words):**
Pompeian mills worked on a simple but effective hourglass principle: a fixed, cone-shaped lower stone (the meta) sat inside a rotating, hopper-shaped upper stone (the catillus). Grain poured into the funnel at the top of the catillus was crushed as it worked down between the two stones, and flour emerged at the base onto a stone channel or lead-lined collecting surface. Larger Roman mills of this "Pompeian mill" type were typically turned by draft animals harnessed to wooden beams fitted into the stone, though smaller or auxiliary mills could also be turned by human (often enslaved) labor; general scholarship notes it was common practice for bakeries across the Roman world to use enslaved people or convicts as mill-turning labor. Direct physical evidence for exactly who or what turned the mills at VII.2.22 specifically — such as worn circular floor grooves — was not identified in the sources checked for this address; but the phenomenon is well documented elsewhere in Pompeii: a December 2023 excavation in Regio IX, Insula 10, uncovered a bakery-mill room with semicircular grooves cut into basalt paving to guide animals' and enslaved workers' circular walking path, and small barred windows sealing the room off from the outside world — described by park director Gabriel Zuchtriegel as "the most shocking side of ancient slavery." The letter-marks cut into VII.2.22's own millstones (up to sixteen abbreviated names) are generally read as quarry or transport marks rather than bakers' stamps. No election notices (programmata), graffiti, or inscriptions naming Popidius Priscus were found recorded inside the bakery itself in sources checked. [Wikipedia — Quern-stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quern-stone); [Wikipedia — Baking in ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baking_in_ancient_Rome); [pompeiisites.org — Prison Bakery press release](https://pompeiisites.org/en/comunicati/pompeii-prison-bakery-emerges/); [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)

## The detail most visitors miss

Look closely at the **millstones themselves for cut letter-marks** — small, abbreviated inscriptions (up to sixteen different name-groups have been recorded here, each about three letters) carved into the basalt. Most visitors read the mills only as generic "grinding stones"; these marks are quarry or transport marks left by the people who cut and moved the volcanic stone from its source (likely the Orvieto area) to Pompeii — a hidden supply-chain record literally carved into the equipment. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)

## Anecdotes

1. **A bakery-prison, found elsewhere in Pompeii, December 2023.** In Regio IX, Insula 10 — a different address from this stop — excavators found a windowless mill room reached only through the house's atrium, its small high windows barred with iron, and the basalt floor around the millstones carved with semicircular grooves to guide the forced, repetitive circling of enslaved workers and donkeys harnessed to the stones. Park director Gabriel Zuchtriegel called it "the most shocking side of ancient slavery, the one devoid of both trusting relationships and promises of manumission." This is not VII.2.22, but it is the best physical evidence anywhere in Pompeii for exactly how mill labor was organized and coerced. [pompeiisites.org — Pompeii: Prison Bakery Emerges](https://pompeiisites.org/en/comunicati/pompeii-prison-bakery-emerges/)
2. **Eighty-one loaves, baked and never eaten.** At the bakery of "Modestus" (VII.1.36, a different bakery a short walk from this one), the oven — found shut with an iron door — still held 81 carbonized loaves, put in to bake minutes before the eruption struck; the finds are held between the Naples Archaeological Museum, the Pompeii site museum, and the Boscoreale Antiquarium. It is the clearest surviving snapshot of a Pompeian bakery interrupted mid-shift, and a useful point of comparison for imagining VII.2.22 in full operation. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.1.36](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2001%2036.htm)
3. **The mills aren't quite where they used to be.** Site documentation for VII.2.22 itself notes that one of the bakery's own millstones appears to have been displaced — resting on modern fill — sometime between the 1970s and 2000s, a small reminder that even in-situ Pompeii is not perfectly frozen in time, but has been shifted by decades of tourism, restoration work, and site management. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.2.22 Part 2](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022%20p2.htm)

## FAQ

**Q: Was this really the bakery of a man named Popidius Priscus?**
A: That is the name used consistently in site documentation for this exact address (VII.2.22) — both pompeiiinpictures.com and Wikipedia's overview of Roman baking independently give the same name for the same regio/insula/doorway. No inscription found inside the bakery itself confirming his ownership was located in this research pass, so treat the name as well-attested but not epigraphically certain. [pompeiiinpictures.com](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2002%2022.htm); [Wikipedia — Baking in ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baking_in_ancient_Rome)

**Q: How many bakeries did Pompeii have?**
A: Wikipedia's overview states there were "at least 31 bakeries in the town, each with wood-burning ovens, millstones and a sales counter" — this bakery was one of that citywide network. [Wikipedia — Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii)

**Q: Did donkeys or enslaved people turn these mills?**
A: Both are attested in Pompeii generally — large mills of this hourglass type were typically animal-powered, and it was common practice to use enslaved or convict labor in bakeries too. For this specific bakery, no floor-groove or skeletal evidence was found in sources checked to say definitively which was used here; the clearest physical proof of forced animal-and-human mill labor comes from a different Pompeii bakery (Regio IX, Insula 10), excavated in 2023. [Wikipedia — Baking in ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baking_in_ancient_Rome); [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/comunicati/pompeii-prison-bakery-emerges/)

**Q: Is any of the actual carbonized bread from this bakery on display?**
A: No — no carbonized loaves specifically from VII.2.22 were identified in sources checked. The famous 81-loaf oven find is from the bakery of Modestus (VII.1.36), a different Pompeii bakery, with finds now split between the Naples Archaeological Museum, the Pompeii site museum, and the Boscoreale Antiquarium. [pompeiiinpictures.com — VII.1.36](http://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R7/7%2001%2036.htm)

## Hebrew naming

**פיסטרינום (pistrinum)** — המונח הלטיני למאפייה-טחנה משולבת; בעברית אפשר לתעתק "פיסטרינום" או לתרגם "מאפייה-טחנה". במדריכים ישראליים בפומפיי נהוג לרוב פשוט "מאפייה רומית" תוך אזכור "פיסטרינום" כמונח המקורי.

**ריחיים בצורת שעון-חול** — התיאור המקובל לטחנות הלבה בפומפיי: אבן עליונה מסתובבת בצורת משפך (קָטִילוּס, catillus) מעל אבן תחתונה קבועה בצורת חרוט (מֶטָה, meta).

**קָטִילוּס ומֶטָה (catillus / meta)** — שמות הלטינית לשתי אבני הריחיים; אין תרגום עברי מקובל, ולכן במדריכים משתמשים במונחים הלטיניים עצמם.

**תנור (tanur)** — התנור המקורי מלבנים, מקושת, שבו נאפה הלחם; מדריכים רבים מציינים שזהו אב-הטיפוס של תנור הפיצה הנאפוליטני המודרני.

**נחתום (nachtom)** — מילה עברית-מקראית-משנאית ל"אופה", המשמשת לעיתים במדריכים לתיאור בעל המאפייה או האופה בה — גם אם המונח הלטיני המדויק הוא pistor.

**חמור טחינה (chamor techina)** — תיאור מקובל לחיית-העבודה שהסתובבה במעגלים כדי להניע את הריחיים; אין במקורות שנבדקו כאן עדות ישירה לחמור באתר VII.2.22 עצמו, אך יש עדות ברורה ממאפייה אחרת בפומפיי (רג'יו IX, איסולה 10), שנחשפה בדצמבר 2023.
