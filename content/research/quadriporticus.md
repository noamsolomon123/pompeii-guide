## What you see

1. A large rectangular open courtyard enclosed by a portico of **74 Doric columns in grey Nocera tufa**, with fluted lower drums originally coated in painted stucco (alternating red-and-yellow shafts, dark red unfluted bases). [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/quadriporticus-of-the-theatres/), [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2007%2016%20p1.htm)
2. A more monumental entrance hall in the north-east corner, marked by **three Ionic columns**, distinct from the plain Doric colonnade around the rest of the courtyard. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2007%2016%20p1.htm)
3. Rows of **small cell-like rooms (roughly 10–15 sq m each)** built on two levels around the perimeter after the AD 62 earthquake conversion — interpreted as gladiators' sleeping quarters, each thought to have housed 2–3 men, with wooden upper galleries (now lost, but their construction is inferred from the archaeology). [BMCR review of Jacobelli, *Gladiators at Pompeii*](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005.07.59/)
4. Graffiti scratched into the plaster of the columns and walls — gladiators' names (e.g. "Celadus" and "Cresces" are recorded among more than 100 inscriptions found on the peristyle), win/loss notations, and dedications. [penelope.uchicago.edu](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/~grout/encyclopaedia_romana/gladiators/quad.html), [BMCR](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005.07.59/)
5. **No original armor is displayed on site today.** The famous parade helmets (some silver-inlaid, decorated with dolphins, tridents, and scenes from the Fall of Troy), greaves, belts, and the retiarius's shoulder-guard found stored in wooden boxes in two rooms here in the winter of 1766–67 were removed long ago and are now in the **Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli (MANN)** — around 50 pieces from this find are part of MANN's permanent weapons collection, and some bear the stamp "NER" / "NER.AUG," marking them as belonging to gladiators of Nero's imperial school at Capua. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2007%2016%20p7.htm), [penelope.uchicago.edu](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/~grout/encyclopaedia_romana/gladiators/quad.html)
6. Evidence of the post-earthquake rebuild: partition walls closing off what was originally an open, walkable portico into the small cells described above — a visible change in construction technique/period from the original elegant Doric portico. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/quadriporticus-of-the-theatres/)
7. A small room on the south side of the courtyard is the findspot of **18 skeletons** recovered in the 1766–67 excavation campaign, including one woman found wearing extensive jewelry — see Anecdotes below. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/quadriporticus-of-the-theatres/)
8. Iron shackles/stocks recovered from one room, read by excavators as evidence of a lock-up (an "ergastulum"-type space) used to restrain enslaved staff or unruly gladiators; one such iron slave shackle is now exhibited in museum collections. [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2007%2016%20p7.htm)

## History in layers

**(a) 30-second intro:** This huge colonnaded square started life as a covered promenade where theatre-goers could stroll or shelter from rain between acts. After an earthquake wrecked much of Pompeii in AD 62, it was rebuilt into cramped barracks and a training ground for gladiators. When Vesuvius erupted in AD 79, the conversion still wasn't finished, and the rooms became a death trap for the people sheltering inside.

**(b) ~150 words:** The quadriporticus was built behind the stage of the Large Theatre in the early-to-mid 1st century BC (estimated around 80–70 BC), following the Hellenistic model — codified by the Roman architect Vitruvius — of a colonnaded courtyard ("porticus post scaenam") where theatre audiences could gather during intervals. [en.wikipedia.org — Theatre Area of Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Area_of_Pompeii), [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadriportico_dei_Teatri) After the earthquake of AD 62 (traditionally dated 5 February AD 62, sometimes given as AD 63 in ancient sources), the structure was substantially restructured and enlarged into a gladiatorial school (ludus) with small cell-rooms, a kitchen, and a dining hall — work that was still incomplete when Vesuvius erupted on 24 August AD 79 (a date now debated by some scholars in favour of an autumn eruption, though this file follows the traditional date pending stop-specific confirmation). [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadriportico_dei_Teatri) The site, along with the neighbouring theatres, was uncovered under Bourbon-era excavations beginning in 1766, with further campaigns in 1774, 1779, 1791–1794, and 1814. [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadriportico_dei_Teatri), [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2007%2016%20p1.htm)

**(c) ~250-word deep-dive:** Whether this space functioned as a true, purpose-built gladiatorial barracks or served a more mixed/civic role remains debated among specialists. The traditional view — supported by the finds of weapons, armor stored in wooden crates, graffiti naming individual fighters, and small dormitory-like cells — is that after AD 62 it became a formal ludus housing as many as roughly 200 gladiators, some scholars link the influx to a boom in gladiator numbers under Nero. [BMCR review of Jacobelli](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005.07.59/) The most cited human find is a set of 18 skeletons recovered from a single room in the 1766–67 dig: among them, a female skeleton wearing rich jewelry (in various accounts: bracelets, rings — one source specifies gemstone rings, including an emerald and a garnet carved with a chariot scene), interpreted in older, romanticized retellings as a "gladiator's jeweled lover" who died with him, though scholars caution the evidence only shows she died in that room, not her relationship to anyone else present — her identity and reason for being there are **not established fact**. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/quadriporticus-of-the-theatres/), [History Collection summary](https://historycollection.com/silent-witnesses-9-astounding-revelations-bodies-discovered-pompeii-volcanic-explosion/6/) Also recovered: remains described by pompeiisites.org as belonging to "four enslaved individuals and 18 free persons" (figures that overlap with, and may refer to the same broader skeletal assemblage as, the "18 skeletons" cited elsewhere — sources are not fully consistent on the exact headcount, flagged as uncertain). [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/quadriporticus-of-the-theatres/) During WWII, Allied bombing aimed at nearby road/rail routes accidentally struck Pompeii; the wall of the Gladiators' Barracks was hit in three places during the September 1943 raids. [Pompeii Perspectives — WWII Bomb Damage](https://www.pompeiiperspectives.org/index.php/wwii-bomb-damage) No specific new (2020–2026) reinterpretation or excavation announcement specific to this building was found in this research pass — flagged as a gap rather than asserted.

## The detail most visitors miss

Look closely at the colonnade itself for the seam between the two building periods: where the original elegant 1st-century-BC Doric intercolumniations (the open spaces between columns) were later **walled up with cruder masonry** to create the small enclosed cell-rooms of the post-AD-62 barracks phase. It's a rare case at Pompeii where you can visually read the exact moment a leisure building became a working (and grim) institutional space — most visitors walk past focused on the open courtyard and miss this construction-technique change in the perimeter walls. [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/quadriporticus-of-the-theatres/)

## Anecdotes

- In the winter of 1766–67, early Bourbon excavators found gladiatorial armor and weapons neatly packed away in wooden boxes in two rooms of the barracks — a greave was logged as found on 20 December 1766, another item on 10 January 1767, showing how meticulously (for the era) the dig was documented. **True, sourced.** [pompeiiinpictures.com](https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R8/8%2007%2016%20p7.htm)
- The same campaign uncovered 18 skeletons in one room, including a woman wearing multiple bracelets and gemstone rings. Older popular accounts turned this into the romantic "legend" of a wealthy woman who died alongside her gladiator lover when Vesuvius erupted — this pairing/relationship is **legend, not established fact**; scholars only confirm she died in the room with others, not who she was or why she was there. **Label: legend for the romantic narrative; the find itself (skeletons + jewelry) is factual.** [pompeiisites.org](https://pompeiisites.org/en/archaeological-site/quadriporticus-of-the-theatres/), [BMCR review of Jacobelli](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005.07.59/)
- During the September 1943 Allied bombing raids intended to disrupt German supply lines during the Salerno landings, the barracks' perimeter wall took three direct hits — one of many accidental WWII scars across the site. **True, sourced.** [Pompeii Perspectives](https://www.pompeiiperspectives.org/index.php/wwii-bomb-damage)

## FAQ

1. **Was this really a gladiator barracks, or just a theatre lobby?** Both, at different times: it was built (c. 80–70 BC, estimated) as a portico for theatre audiences, then converted into a gladiatorial school and living quarters after the AD 62 earthquake. [it.wikipedia.org](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadriportico_dei_Teatri)
2. **Where is the gladiator armor found here now?** Almost all of it — helmets, greaves, belts, a retiarius shoulder-guard, some stamped with Nero's imperial gladiator school mark — was moved to the Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli (MANN) and is not on display at Pompeii itself. [penelope.uchicago.edu](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/~grout/encyclopaedia_romana/gladiators/quad.html)
3. **Who was the "jeweled woman" found here?** Unknown. A skeleton found among 18 others in one room wore rich jewelry, which sparked romantic speculation that she was a gladiator's lover — but this is unverified; her identity and relationship to the site's residents is not established. [History Collection](https://historycollection.com/silent-witnesses-9-astounding-revelations-bodies-discovered-pompeii-volcanic-explosion/6/)
4. **Can you still see gladiator graffiti here today?** Yes — inscriptions including gladiator names such as "Celadus" and "Cresces," among more than 100 graffiti recorded on the columns and walls of the portico. [penelope.uchicago.edu](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/~grout/encyclopaedia_romana/gladiators/quad.html), [BMCR](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005.07.59/)

## Hebrew naming

- קוודריפורטיקו של התיאטראות / הקזרמה של הגלדיאטורים — *Kvadriportiko shel ha-te'atra'ot / ha-Kazarma shel ha-Gladiatorim* (site name; Israeli guides commonly just say "קזרמת הגלדיאטורים" — Kazarmat ha-Gladiatorim).
- גלדיאטור — *Gladiator* (same word, standard Hebrew usage — Latin "gladiator").
- לודוס (בית ספר לגלדיאטורים) — *Ludus* (training school for gladiators; transliterated directly, sometimes explained in Hebrew as "בית ספר לגלדיאטורים").
- פריסטיל / קוודריפורטיקו — *Peristil / Kvadriportiko* (peristyle/quadriporticus — the colonnaded courtyard; "פריסטיל" is the more commonly used Hebrew architectural term with Israeli guides, "קוודריפורטיקו" used for this specific four-sided form).
- פלסטרה — *Palestra* (exercise/training ground; note this building itself is not formally a "palaestra" — the nearby Samnite Palaestra is a separate structure — but the term is often loosely applied by guides to describe the gladiators' training use of this courtyard).
